The prior authorization problem

Prior authorization affects 38 million Americans annually. Every week, patients wait for procedures their physicians have already decided they need.

🏥
Care delayed — sometimes abandoned
A patient needs a knee replacement. Surgery is scheduled. The PA request sits in a queue — manual policy lookup, chart review, documentation gathering. The OR slot may be gone before the answer arrives.
1 in 4
patients abandon treatment due to PA burden (AMA 2023)
📋
Hours of administrative work, per case
Each PA request requires staff to gather records, format documentation, submit to the payer portal, and follow up — repeatedly. Physicians' offices spend an average of 14 hours per week on PA alone.
14 hrs
per physician per week on PA administration (AMA)
⚖️
Inconsistent decisions
Manual review quality varies by reviewer, shift, and daily caseload. The same clinical facts can produce different determinations. Neither providers nor patients can predict the outcome.
35%
of PA appeals are overturned — meaning the first decision was wrong (KFF 2023)

One rule the AI never breaks: it does not recommend denial.

The agent can approve a case, or it can identify insufficient evidence and specify exactly what is missing. The denial decision — with its regulatory implications, appeal rights, and potential impact on patient care — belongs to the human reviewer alone.

This is not a limitation. A drafted denial anchors human reviewers toward rejection. Framing unmet criteria as evidence gaps preserves reviewer objectivity and keeps the human in the right role.

The human reviewer's experience

When a case routes to human review, the AI does not hand off a pile of records. It hands off a briefing.

The utilization management nurse does not start from scratch. The AI has already read the chart, checked every criterion, cited its evidence, and explained exactly what is missing. The reviewer's job is to exercise clinical judgment — not to redo the documentation work.

AI RATIONALE

Criteria c1 (BMI ≥ 40) and c3 (psychological evaluation) are met with valid clinical citations. Criterion c2 — completion of a 6-month physician-supervised dietary program — has no supporting documentation in the patient chart. The AI cannot confirm this criterion is met and has not inferred or fabricated evidence to suggest otherwise.

Evidence gap: 6-month supervised dietary program documentation is absent from the chart. Records from a referring provider or a structured weight-management program would satisfy this criterion.

Denial is the reviewer's decision alone. The AI never recommends denial.

⚖️
Regulatory accountability
Denials carry appeal rights and regulatory obligations under ACA and state law. That decision — and its documentation — must be owned by a licensed clinician, not an AI system.
🔍
Context the chart does not capture
A patient may have completed a diet program through an out-of-network provider whose records are not in the FHIR chart. The reviewer can request that information before making a final call.
🧑‍⚕️
Clinical judgment on edge cases
Policy criteria do not cover every clinical scenario. A reviewer can weigh factors — comorbidity severity, patient-specific risk — that a policy document never anticipated.
